What the Spectator Actually Published

Measuring Jewish- and Israel-related coverage in the Columbia Daily Spectator, and testing whether “antisemitism” in that coverage rose — under two rival definitions that disagree.

Read this before the charts. The request was 1950 onward. That is not what this measures, and the gap is not a detail.

Columbia’s historical Spectator archive — the only full-text source for 1950–2012 — sits behind a Cloudflare bot challenge that returns 403 to every request. The Wayback Machine holds only 14 text-mode captures of it. No pre-2013 text was obtainable, so no pre-2013 result is reported here. Everything below comes from the paper’s modern CMS, whose index runs out at roughly 2013 and is only dense from 2017.

The two measures, side by side

This is the whole point of the exercise. There is no agreed operational definition of antisemitism, and the two leading frameworks disagree in a way that matters enormously for a campus paper:

  • Narrow (consensus). Classic antisemitism aimed at Jews as Jews — conspiracy and control tropes, dual loyalty, Holocaust denial, dehumanization, Nazi symbols, exclusion, collective blame. The IHRA definition and the Jerusalem Declaration both count these.
  • IHRA-extended (contested). Adds IHRA’s Israel-related examples — denying Jewish self-determination, Zionism-as-racism, Nazi comparisons, apartheid and genocide framing. The Jerusalem Declaration explicitly excludes these, treating them as political speech about a state.

Reporting on it vs. saying it

A newspaper that covers a swastika found in a dormitory contains the word “swastika” and a great deal of negative language about Jews. It is not thereby antisemitic — it is doing journalism. Naive sentiment analysis gets this exactly backwards, and would turn good coverage of a hate incident into evidence of hatred.

So every flagged passage is classified by stance from cues in a ±400-character window: condemned (the passage sits in critical or condemnatory coverage), reported (quoted, attributed, or describing graffiti and chants), or unattributed (no attribution and no condemnation nearby — the closest this method gets to the paper’s own voice).

What was measured, exactly

Corpus

Articles were pulled from the Spectator’s Arc Publishing content API across eight sections (news, opinion, sports, arts & entertainment, The Eye, Spectrum, city news, multimedia), paginating each to the index’s depth limit. The API caps out near 9,900 items per section, which is what pins the earliest usable year to roughly 2013. Full body text was taken from each article’s structured content elements, deduplicated by canonical URL. robots.txt permits this crawl.

Two precision gates

An earlier version of the lexicon matched bare phrases like “control the media” and “run the world.” Manual review of all 13 of its own-voice hits found 13 false positives and zero true positives — an endowed “Rothschild professor” chair, “undivided loyalty” boilerplate from a trustee conflict-of-interest policy, “running media campaigns” about election advertising, the marathon “running world,” and “Deps run the world” from a staff acknowledgments column. That is a precision of 0%, and it is why two gates now apply:

  • Subject gate. Every power-and-control and dual-loyalty pattern requires an explicit Jewish or Zionist subject in the same sentence as the predicate.
  • Anchor gate. A match is discarded unless a Jewish or Israel anchor term also appears in the surrounding window.

Against the eight known false positives and seven held-out true positives, the revised lexicon produces 0 false positives and 0 misses.

Limitations

  • The period requested is mostly absent. 1950–2012 is missing entirely. Anyone wanting the postwar Jewish-quota era, 1968, or the 1970s–2000s Israel debates needs archive access this analysis did not have.
  • 2013–2016 is too thin to rate. Those years return a handful of articles each and are excluded from every rate; they are listed in the table above so the sparsity is visible rather than smoothed over.
  • Lexicons detect topics, not intent. A pattern match is evidence that language appeared, never proof of an author’s meaning. Precision is reported, not assumed.
  • Stance cues are shallow. “Denounced” fires the condemnation rule whoever is doing the denouncing — so a sentence about protesters denouncing Israel can be misread as the paper condemning antisemitism. This inflates “condemned” in the IHRA-extended family especially.
  • Sentiment is not antisemitism. VADER measures valence. Coverage of a war is negative regardless of any animus, which is exactly why the tone chart is kept separate from the flag counts and never summed with them.
  • Section mix shifts. The index returns different amounts per section per year, so the denominator is not a constant sample of the paper. Rates per 100 topic-relevant articles are reported alongside raw counts for this reason.
  • The IHRA-extended series is a definitional artifact as much as an empirical one. It rises when Israel-related political speech rises. Whether that constitutes antisemitism is precisely the contested question — this analysis quantifies the choice rather than settling it.
