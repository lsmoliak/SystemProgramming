Battle setup

How the math works

1. One roll

The attacker rolls na = min(3, a) dice and the defender rolls nd = min(2, d). Both hands are sorted high to low and compared pair by pair, so m = min(na, nd) armies are lost in total. These odds never change, so they are fixed constants (counted from all 6na+nd equally likely dice combinations):

2. The whole battle

A battle is a Markov chain on states (a, d). Every roll removes 1 or 2 armies, so a + d always drops and the chain can't loop. Let pk be the chance the attacker loses k armies on one roll. Then each state moves to (a − k, d − (m − k)) with probability pk.

The chance of finishing in each end state comes from pushing probability forward from the start:

π(A, D) = 1
π(a − k, d − m + k) += π(a, d) · p_k(n_a, n_d)
     for every live state (a ≥ 1, d ≥ 1), largest a + d first

P(attacker wins with j left)  = π(j, 0)    j = 1 … A
P(defender holds with j left) = π(0, j)    j = 1 … D

The same chain gives the win chance as a backward recurrence, which is handy for building lookup tables:

W(a, 0) = 1,   W(0, d) = 0
W(a, d) = Σ_k p_k(n_a, n_d) · W(a − k, d − m + k)

The expected armies left are the averages over the end states: E[attackers] = Σ j·π(j, 0) and E[defenders] = Σ j·π(0, j). Each state is visited once, so the work grows with A × D. Even 100 v 100 finishes in a few milliseconds. The answers are exact, with no simulation.
