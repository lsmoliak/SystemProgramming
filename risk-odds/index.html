How to use

  • Enter the armies that can attack. Leave out the 1 that must stay behind, so a territory with 6 armies attacks with 5.
  • Defending territories is a chain the attacker takes one after another. Every territory holds at least 1 army, so defending armies must be at least the number of territories. The extra armies all sit on one territory. Stack: First puts them on the first territory attacked and Last on the final one.
  • Each conquered territory keeps 1 attacking army as a garrison, the last one included. The rest move on.
  • The attack runs until every territory falls or the attacker has no armies left to attack with.
  • Each roll uses the most dice allowed: attacker up to 3, defender up to 2. A 1-army territory rolls 1 die. Defender wins ties.
  • Spare attackers are the armies still free to keep attacking after every garrison is placed. The line under it shows armies lost to dice, then the average spare when the attack wins.
  • Defenders left counts every defending army still on the board, across all territories. The line under it shows armies lost, then the average number of territories the defender still holds.
  • Net armies is the defender's expected losses to dice minus the attacker's. Garrisons aren't counted as losses. A positive number means the attack takes more armies off the board than it costs.

One roll

The attacker rolls na = min(3, a) dice and the defender rolls nd = min(2, d). Both hands are sorted high to low and compared pair by pair, so m = min(na, nd) armies are lost in total. These odds are fixed constants, counted from all 6na+nd equally likely dice combinations:

The whole battle

A battle is a Markov chain on states (a, d). Every roll removes 1 or 2 armies, so a + d always drops and the chain can't loop. Let pk be the chance the attacker loses k armies on one roll. Then each state moves to (a − k, d − (m − k)) with probability pk.

π(A, D) = 1
π(a − k, d − m + k) += π(a, d) · p_k(n_a, n_d)
     for every live state (a ≥ 1, d ≥ 1), largest a + d first

P(attacker wins with j left)  = π(j, 0)    j = 1 … A
P(defender holds with j left) = π(0, j)    j = 1 … D

The win chance as a backward recurrence:

W(a, 0) = 1,   W(0, d) = 0
W(a, d) = Σ_k p_k(n_a, n_d) · W(a − k, d − m + k)

Expected values come from the end states:

E[attackers left] = Σ j · π(j, 0)
E[defenders left] = Σ j · π(0, j)
Net = (D − E[defenders left]) − (A − E[attackers left])

The work grows with A × D, so even 100 v 100 finishes in a few milliseconds. The answers are exact, with no simulation.

A chain of territories

The defender holds territories 1 … T with d1, …, dT armies, attacked in that order. Let μt(a) be the chance the attacker arrives at territory t with a attacking armies. Because the one-territory chain above is linear, it can start from the whole distribution at once. Seed π(a, dt) = μt(a) for every a and run the same forward pass. Its end states give the next arrival distribution, after 1 army stays behind as a garrison:

μ_1(A) = 1
π_t(a, d_t) = μ_t(a)            for every a, then forward pass
μ_{t+1}(j − 1) = π_t(j, 0)       j ≥ 1   (1 garrisons territory t)

P(take all, s spare)          = μ_{T+1}(s)
P(defender keeps k armies)    = Σ_t π_t(0, k − R_t),   R_t = d_{t+1} + … + d_T
E[territories taken]          = Σ_t Σ_j π_t(j, 0)
Net = (D − E[defenders left]) − (A − E[spare] − E[territories taken])

Arriving with 0 armies is the state (0, dt), so it lands straight in the defender's column and needs no special case. A 1-army territory always rolls nd = 1, because nd = min(2, d). The cost is A × (D + T) in total.

Front vs back. Front-loaded means d = (D−T+1, 1, …, 1) and back-loaded means d = (1, …, 1, D−T+1). The order matters. Back-loaded, the attacker leaves garrisons first and reaches the big stack with fewer armies and fewer 3-dice rolls. So a back-loaded stack is usually harder to break.
